## Abstract

The design of Emirati characters for stylized 3D game environments faces a documented tension between cultural authenticity and visual system coherence, a tension that has historically resolved in favor of stylization, producing ethnic erasure as its documented result. This article asks what design criteria, grounded in empirical evidence on UAE cultural identity, should guide the development of Emirati characters in stylized 3D environments to prevent ethnic erasure without compromising the coherence of an established visual system. The empirical object is the UAE Female character developed for Endstar, a collaborative 3D game-making platform by Endless Studios and E-Line Media. The method is qualitative bibliographic and documentary research with cross-domain analytical synthesis across three peer-reviewed domains. Three theoretical lenses orient the analysis: Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model, Edward Said's critique of orientalism, and the preservation-amplification principle derived from cross-cultural animation studies. The hypothesis states that preservation and amplification of documented ethnic markers constitutes the methodologically grounded approach to this problem. Three convergent evidential pillars support the hypothesis: Emirati facial morphology, with emphasis on the nasal profile documented as *sallat al saif*; traditional dress as a semiotic system of identity demarcation; and the design precedent established by the animated series *Freej* (2006). Four operational design criteria are derived from the converging evidence and translated into production guidelines for the Endstar pipeline. The methodology developed here offers a transferable model for any context in which a non-dominant cultural identity is being translated into interactive media.

*Keywords:* character design, Emirati cultural identity, game art, stylization, ethnic representation, encoding/decoding, UAE

## Between Identity and Stylization: Evidence-Based Criteria for Representing Emirati Characters in Stylized 3D Game Environments

Character design decisions encode a position on what a community looks like. Every choice about which features to simplify, retain, or discard produces a specific visual claim about a specific group of people. When designers make those choices without grounding in documented cultural evidence, the result reproduces whatever assumptions the designer brought to the process. Hall (1980) established that media producers encode meanings into cultural products through deliberate sign selection, and that those selections are never neutral. Said (1978) demonstrated that Western cultural production has historically constructed non-Western communities through typologies serving ideological functions disconnected from the communities themselves. Vít Šisler (2008) extended this analysis to video games, showing that Arab and Muslim characters in Western productions systematically reproduce orientalist convention rather than documented cultural reality.

This article addresses a specific instance of that problem: the development of Emirati female characters for Endstar, a stylized 3D collaborative game-making platform developed by Endless Studios and E-Line Media, deployed in active educational programming with UAE students (Endless Studios, 2024a). The Endstar visual system is a fully codified aesthetic framework with an established roster of stylized characters (Endless Studios, 2024a, 2024b). New characters must cohere visually with that roster. Emirati characters must also read as specifically Emirati to an Emirati audience. These two requirements create a design tension that demands explicit methodological resolution.

Two factors documented in the literature amplify this tension. First, Emiratis constitute approximately 20% of the UAE population, surrounded by expatriate communities from over one hundred nationalities (Khalaf, 2005). Visual identity markers are not decorative in this context. They are active instruments of cultural boundary maintenance. A character that collapses Emirati features into a generic pan-Arab aesthetic does not occupy neutral representational ground; it erases a distinction the community itself maintains at significant social cost. Second, the UAE audience has been shaped by sustained exposure to Japanese anime and American studio animation, establishing production quality expectations that a culturally specific character must also meet (Tariq & Willard, 2022). Cultural accuracy without visual quality does not pass the test.

The pattern documented in the representation literature shows that this tension has historically resolved in favor of stylization, with ethnic erasure as the result (Šisler, 2008; Al-Rawi, 2024; Alghamdi, 2024). That resolution is structural, not accidental. It follows from a design process that begins from orientalist convention rather than from documented cultural evidence (Said, 1978; Šisler, 2008). The present article proposes a methodological alternative.

The central research question is the following: what design criteria, grounded in empirical evidence on UAE cultural identity, should guide the development of Emirati characters in stylized 3D environments to prevent ethnic erasure without compromising the coherence of an established visual system? The hypothesis states that preservation and amplification of documented ethnic markers, as opposed to their substitution by generic cartoon conventions, constitutes the methodologically grounded approach to this problem. Three domains of evidence are examined to test this hypothesis: facial morphology and perceptual science (Wang et al., 2015; Alshehhi et al., 2025), traditional dress as a semiotic system (Khalaf, 2005; Goto, 2016), and the design precedent established by the animated series *Freej* (Ercegovac et al., 2025; Tariq & Willard, 2022). The article tests the hypothesis through cross-domain evidential synthesis and translates the findings into operational design criteria for the Endstar production context.

## Theoretical Framework

***Encoding/Decoding.*** Stuart Hall (1980) argued that media producers encode meanings into cultural products through deliberate sign selection. Audiences decode those meanings from their own cultural positions. Decoding is not passive reception. Decoding is an active construction shaped by the cultural competencies of the viewer. Hall identified three decoding positions: dominant-hegemonic, where the viewer accepts the encoded meaning; negotiated, where the viewer partially accepts and partially resists; and oppositional, where the viewer reads against the grain of intended meaning.

Applied to character design, Hall's model reframes the problem as a communication problem. The designer encodes Emirati identity through visual sign selection: facial features, dress, proportions, and skin tone. The Emirati viewer decodes those signs through cultural competencies built from lived experience. Wang et al. (2015) documented that Emiratis outperform American participants in facial recognition tasks, with the strongest advantage concentrated in the nasal region. As shown in Figure 2, the cultural practice of covering the upper head in public frames the face around the nose, directing sustained perceptual attention to that region across a lifetime. Emiratis are trained decoders of precisely the facial feature that character design conventions least emphasize. A nose that does not carry the nasal signals associated with Emirati identity produces, at minimum, a negotiated decoding, and more likely an oppositional one.

***Cultural Representation in Stylized Animation.*** Edward Said (1978) established that Western cultural production has historically constructed the Arab and Muslim Other through recurring typologies serving ideological functions disconnected from the communities represented. Vít Šisler (2008) extended this analysis to video games. Arab and Muslim characters in Western productions are systematically reduced to archetypes reflecting orientalist tradition rather than ethnographic observation. Ahmed Al-Rawi (2024) traced this pattern longitudinally. The dominant mode of Middle Eastern representation in games shifted from romantically exotic to militantly threatening following geopolitical developments, without passing through accurate at any point. The pattern is structural, not incidental.

This lens clarifies what is at stake in the Endstar context. An Emirati character built from orientalist assumptions does not represent a failure of craft. It reproduces a documented pattern of harm. Bushra Alfaraj (2019) provided the only available empirical data on how Arab gamers experience this pattern. Interviews with 81 Arab gamers documented consistent oppositional readings of Arab characters in mainstream games. Participants described the experience of seeing their culture reduced to antagonist backgrounds as alienating and dismissive. Figure 3 illustrates two documented examples of this pattern in commercially released games.

***The Preservation-Amplification Principle.*** Ercegovac et al. (2025) identified in Disney's *Moana* (2016) a design methodology that resolves the apparent conflict between cultural specificity and cartoon stylization. The production team built the character's cartoon appeal outward from Polynesian facial morphology. Cultural specificity and stylization were not treated as opposing demands. The two were constructed simultaneously from the same geometry. Ethnic markers were not reduced to fit the cartoon template. They were translated into cartoon geometry that reads as those markers at the required level of abstraction.

This third lens provides what the other two do not: an operational model. Hall (1980) identifies the communication stakes. The representation of literature identifies the failure of modes. The preservation-amplification principle identifies the method. Applied to Emirati characters in Endstar, the directive is concrete: begin from documented Emirati ethnic features and work outward toward stylization, not the reverse.

## Method

The design of Emirati characters for an established stylized visual system cannot be validated by cultural intuition alone. It requires documented evidence, and documented evidence requires a systematic method for locating, extracting, and triangulating findings across domains.

***Empirical Object.*** The empirical object of this study is the UAE Female character developed for Endstar (Endless Studios, 2024a, 2024b), a stylized 3D Emirati female figure wearing the traditional burqa facial mask, Abuteela dress, and shayla head covering, as shown in Figure 1. The author produced the character as Senior 3D Character Artist at E-Line Media. The character functions simultaneously as a cultural text encoding specific visual claims about Emirati identity, and as a production artifact resulting from decisions made within specific institutional and aesthetic constraints. The analytical delimitation is precise: this study addresses the design criteria that should govern the production of such a character and tests those criteria against the character as realized. Audience reception and broader game production context fall outside the scope of this analysis.

***Method and Technique.*** The method is qualitative bibliographic and documentary research with cross-domain analytical synthesis (Creswell & Creswell, 2018; Merriam & Tisdell, 2016). Literature was retrieved from academic databases including Google Scholar, ResearchGate, PubMed, and institutional repositories, using search terms combining "Emirati," "UAE," "character design," "Arab representation," "facial morphology," and "stylized animation." Three domains of peer-reviewed literature were systematically reviewed: facial morphology and perceptual science, with emphasis on UAE population studies (Wang et al., 2015; Alshehhi et al., 2025); cultural sociology, with emphasis on Emirati dress and identity construction (Khalaf, 2005; Goto, 2016); and animation and game studies, with emphasis on Arab representation and ethnic stylization precedents (Šisler, 2008; Al-Rawi, 2024; Ercegovac et al., 2025). Evidence was extracted from each domain and assessed for direct implications on specific design decisions. Findings were triangulated across domains to identify convergent and divergent conclusions (Merriam & Tisdell, 2016).

***Methodological Justification.*** This method was chosen because the research question is normative: it asks what criteria should govern a design practice, not what criteria currently do (Creswell & Creswell, 2018). A reception study would require access to Emirati test audiences unavailable within this research scope. An ethnographic study would require extended fieldwork in the UAE, equally outside the available resources. Qualitative bibliographic and documentary research allows the construction of an evidence base that directly addresses the morphological, cultural, and artistic dimensions of the problem within the available scope (Merriam & Tisdell, 2016).

***Methodological Limits.*** Two limits must be declared. The criteria proposed here are informed by indirect evidence of cultural recognition, including perceptual science data, sociological analysis, and the validated precedent of *Freej*, rather than by direct empirical testing with Emirati viewers of the Endstar character. Whether the character passes the recognition test with the target audience remains an open question resolvable only through a reception study (Merriam & Tisdell, 2016). The evidence base also reflects available literature as of early 2026. Research on Emirati facial morphology is limited to two major studies (Wang et al., 2015; Alshehhi et al., 2025). Within the scope of the databases consulted for this study, no peer-reviewed research was identified that addresses Emirati character design in games as a dedicated field of inquiry, a gap that Ercegovac et al. (2025) also acknowledge in the adjacent field of Emirati animation studies.

## Results

***Arab Character Representation in Games: Evidence from the Literature.*** The representation of Arab characters in Western game media has been studied across three decades of production. The findings converge on a consistent pattern with a consistent mechanism.

Vít Šisler (2008) analyzed Arab and Muslim representation across a broad sample of Western video games and identified that representation decisions were made from audience preference modeling oriented toward Western markets, not from cultural research. The result was the reproduction of cultural stereotypes that confirmed existing assumptions rather than engaged with documented cultural reality. Ahmed Al-Rawi (2024) extended this analysis longitudinally, identifying four recurring representational themes across four decades of game production: romantic exoticism, desert primitivism, oil-wealth caricature, and militant threat. Each theme emerged from geopolitical moments rather than from any engagement with the communities being represented.

Fayez Alghamdi (2024) brought this analysis to specific design examples, as shown in Figure 3. The concept art for Shaheen in Tekken 7 assembled orientalist signifiers without documented knowledge of how Gulf Arab men dress in any context. Arab players read the character as inauthentic on sight. Zafina in Tekken 6 was described by Bushra Alfaraj (2019) as a female Arab character whose costume combined hypersexualized elements from multiple unrelated cultures into a figure positioned as Arab by name alone. Both cases share the same mechanism: designers worked from convention when evidence was required.

Alfaraj (2019) provided the only available empirical data on how Arab gamers experience this pattern. Interviews with 81 Arab gamers across multiple countries documented consistent oppositional readings of Arab characters in mainstream games. Participants described seeing their culture reduced to antagonist backgrounds as alienating and dismissive.

The specific risk for Emirati characters is compounded by one factor the broader literature does not fully address. Emirati identity is not interchangeable with a generic Arab or MENA identity. The design patterns documented by Šisler (2008), Al-Rawi (2024), and Alghamdi (2024) produce characters coded as vaguely Middle Eastern rather than specifically anything. An Emirati viewer looking at a generic Arab character does not see imperfect representation of Emirati culture. An Emirati viewer sees someone else's culture, misattributed.

***Emirati Facial Morphology: The Evidence Base.*** Two peer-reviewed studies provide the primary evidential base on Emirati facial morphology. The findings are convergent and the implications for character design are direct.

Wang et al. (2015) documented that Emiratis significantly outperform American participants in facial recognition tasks, with the strongest advantage concentrated in the nasal region. As shown in Figure 2, the cultural practice of covering the upper head in public frames the face around the nose and lower facial area, directing sustained perceptual attention to that region across a lifetime. Emiratis are trained readers of nasal morphology. The specific nasal profile functioning as a primary cultural identity marker in Emirati discourse is expressed through the Arabic phrase *sallat al saif*, meaning like a drawn sword. The morphological characteristics this phrase encodes are a straight nasal bridge without dorsal hump or concavity, a narrow alar base, and a pointed nasal tip with a downward nasolabial angle. This profile appears in traditional greetings, in poetry, and in daily discourse about beauty and kinship.

Alshehhi et al. (2025) provided the biological grounding for this observation. As shown in Figure 4, the genome-wide association study recruited 172 Emirati participants and obtained 44 facial measurements through three-dimensional scanning and automatic landmarking. The analysis identified 19 genetic loci associated with six primary facial features. Orbital protrusion and nasolabial angle appeared as genetically significant markers in the Emirati population. Orbital protrusion, the degree to which the eye globe sits forward relative to the orbital rim, is the biological correlate of socket depth in character art. Socket depth is the visible shadow relationship between the orbital rim and the eye globe that makes a face read as dimensional rather than flat. The nasolabial angle finding directly encodes the downward nasal tip orientation of *sallat al saif* at the genetic level.

As shown in Figure 5, additional morphological characteristics documented in the ethnographic literature include an oval-to-round face shape in which the jaw recedes from the silhouette and the cheekbones are present but do not dominate. The eye is large and horizontally elongated with a defined outer corner. The lacrimal caruncle, the small tissue at the inner corner of the eye, is an anatomical detail whose presence or absence significantly affects ethnic specificity in stylized facial geometry.

***Traditional Dress as Semiotic System.*** Traditional Emirati dress carries a semiotic function of identity demarcation in a demographically specific and politically charged social field.

Sulayman Khalaf (2005) conducted five years of ethnographic fieldwork on the use of national dress by Emirati men and women. The central finding is precise: traditional dress functions as a boundary marker, a visible signal demarcating group membership in a contested social field. In a country where nationals constitute approximately 20% of the population, surrounded by expatriate communities from over one hundred nationalities, wearing traditional Emirati dress is a public assertion of identity, belonging, and distinction within a social environment where that identity requires active maintenance. Khalaf documented the emotional intensity of this relationship through a specific incident: when Disney characters appeared at the opening of a McDonald's franchise in Dubai wearing imitations of Emirati national dress, two Emirati men attacked them for what they read as an insult to their national costume. The stakes of visual identity markers are not theoretical for this community.

Manami Goto (2016) added a historical dimension bearing directly on the design of traditional or elderly Emirati female characters. Photographic and ethnographic evidence documents that the burqa, the rigid metallic face mask covering the nose and mouth while leaving the eyes and cheekbones visible, was the primary female garment of the pre-oil period in the UAE. The widespread adoption of the black abaya as the standard female outer garment occurred in the 1970s and 1980s. For an elderly female character, the burqa is historically accurate in a way the abaya is not.

The hierarchy of Emirati female visual identity markers, in descending order of cultural recognition function, is the following. The burqa is the single most unambiguous marker: a rigid mask in shiny gold or metallic fabric, covering the nose, mouth, and chin, with the eyes and cheekbones visible above. The Abuteela is a long, richly colored dress in deep greens, reds, golds, or whites with intricate embroidery along the neckline, sleeves, and hem. The shayla is a wide black scarf worn over the head covering the hair. Henna decoration on the hands, with circular motifs on the palms and colored fingertips, provides additional cultural specificity.

***Freej as Solved Precedent.*** The animated series *Freej*, created by Mohammed Saeed Harib and first broadcast in 2006, occupies a unique position in this analysis. Within the scope of the literature reviewed, it is the only existing 3D animated production to have solved, to a validated degree, the design problem under examination. The series features four elderly traditional Emirati women navigating the tensions between traditional life and rapid modernization in contemporary Dubai. Its nearly two decades of cultural presence in the Gulf region constitutes empirical evidence that the character design solutions passed the recognition test with the target audience (Ercegovac et al., 2025; Tariq & Willard, 2022).

Harib's methodology began with extensive research into Emirati poetry, dialect, and cultural heritage before any character design decisions were made (Ercegovac et al., 2025). The primary stated objective was the protection of Emirati cultural heritage, specifically the preservation of old Emirati poetic forms and phrases disappearing from daily use. The cultural evidence preceded the visual decisions. This sequence is the methodological principle the present article proposes.

Ercegovac et al. (2025) analyzed *Freej* through content analysis and identified visual exaggeration as the primary technique through which the series communicated cultural specificity. The key distinction the analysis establishes is between exaggeration and caricature. Caricature distorts features for comic or critical effect, typically at the cost of dignity. Exaggeration amplifies features carrying cultural significance so those features read clearly at the level of abstraction the medium requires. The burqa profile was simplified into a graphic shape remaining immediately legible without photorealistic material simulation. The eye region, the only facial area visible above the burqa, was enlarged and given strong brow expressivity to carry the full weight of emotional communication. The Abuteela dress was translated into animation-ready geometry preserving the graphic identity of the embroidery pattern while meeting production constraints.

Tariq and Willard (2022) provided the audience context explaining why these design decisions were necessary. The UAE audience brings sophisticated visual expectations shaped by consumption of Japanese anime and American studio animation. A character reading as culturally authentic but visually inferior to international production standards will not be accepted on the basis of cultural recognition alone. Rima Almarzouqi (2022, as cited in Ercegovac et al., 2025), writing as an Emirati viewer, confirmed that the design details of *Freej*, including the Abuteela, the burqa, and the henna decoration, are culturally precise and recognizable as representations of the Emirati grandmother generation.

## Discussion

The hypothesis advanced in this article receives support from the convergence of evidence across three research domains. Each domain contributes a distinct and non-redundant argument.

The perceptual science data establish that Emirati viewers bring trained perceptual attention to the nasal region of a face (Wang et al., 2015). The nasal profile associated with Emirati cultural identity, sallat al saif, is documented both culturally and genetically (Wang et al., 2015; Alshehhi et al., 2025). A character design that softens or generalizes this profile produces a face that Emirati viewers read as not belonging to their community, regardless of the quality of other elements (Wang et al., 2015). The data do not require realistic rendering. They require that the nasal profile carry the correct visual signals at whatever level of abstraction the stylized system operates.

The sociological data establish that traditional Emirati dress functions as a semiotic system of active identity demarcation (Khalaf, 2005; Goto, 2016). A character wearing these markers correctly will produce a dominant-hegemonic decoding in Hall's (1980) terms. A character wearing them incorrectly or not at all will produce an oppositional one. The historical evidence from Goto (2016) adds the finding that for elderly female characters, the burqa is the historically appropriate primary marker rather than the abaya.

The animation precedent data establish that cultural specificity and stylized appeal are not in structural opposition (Ercegovac et al., 2025). Freej resolved both simultaneously through a methodology that began with cultural research and worked outward toward stylized geometry (Ercegovac et al., 2025). The series' nearly two decades of cultural presence in the Gulf region constitutes the strongest available evidence that this methodology produces valid results with Emirati audiences (Ercegovac et al., 2025; Tariq & Willard, 2022). The hypothesis is supported by triangulation of evidence across three peer-reviewed domains. As with any hypothesis based on indirect evidence, it remains open to refinement as new studies on Emirati facial morphology and cultural representation are published.

From this evidential convergence, four operational design criteria emerge for the Endstar production context. Each criterion is derived directly from the findings, not from intuition or convention. Figure 7 shows the UAE Female character as the application of these criteria within the Endstar visual system.

The first criterion addresses nasal morphology. The sallat al saif profile, with its straight bridge, narrow alar base, and pointed tip with downward nasolabial angle, must be present and legible at the Endstar visual system's level of stylization (Wang et al., 2015; Alshehhi et al., 2025). The appropriate operation is translation, not reduction. The feature is simplified into cartoon geometry that reads as this specific nose, not softened into a generic cartoon nose placed on an Emirati character (Ercegovac et al., 2025).

The second criterion addresses orbital structure. Socket depth must be legible. The eyelid rim must be constructed with visible thickness. The shadow relationship between the orbital rim and the eye globe must be readable at game distances (Alshehhi et al., 2025). This criterion is simultaneously a cultural accuracy requirement, supported by the genetic data on orbital protrusion, and a craft requirement applicable to any stylized character intended to read as dimensional rather than flat.

The third criterion addresses costuming. The burqa, where contextually appropriate, must be rendered as a rigid metallic mask in gold tones, with its characteristic curved lower profile legible as a graphic shape at game distances (Khalaf, 2005; Goto, 2016). The Abuteela must present as a richly colored garment with clear embroidery distribution along the neckline, sleeves, and hem (Khalaf, 2005). These are not decorative choices. They are the visual signs through which Emirati identity is encoded in the character and decoded by Emirati viewers (Hall, 1980).

The fourth criterion addresses calibration within the established Endstar visual system (Endless Studios, 2024a, 2024b). A new Emirati character that reads as more realistic than the existing Endstar roster requires a stylization pass. A character that reads as ethnically generic, without legible sallat al saif morphology or correct costuming, requires a cultural accuracy pass (Wang et al., 2015; Khalaf, 2005). The two calibrations are not opposed. The design goal is a character simultaneously more stylized than realistic and more specifically Emirati than generic. Mohammad Lataifeh and colleagues (2024) observed that designers working from memory and convention rather than external evidential input are susceptible to mental fixation, an unconscious adherence to previous solutions that constrains the generative process. The research-first methodology this article proposes is, among other things, a response to that documented cognitive constraint (Lataifeh et al., 2024). Figure 8 shows the Endstar character roster, illustrating the established visual system within which the UAE Female character was developed and against which all calibration is measured.

## Conclusion

This article proposed an evidence-based framework for the design of Emirati characters within the stylized 3D visual system of Endstar. The answer to the research question emerges from the convergence of three empirical pillars. The first establishes that the nose is the primary ethnic marker in Emirati visual identity and that the *sallat al saif* profile is both culturally documented and genetically specific to the Emirati population (Wang et al., 2015; Alshehhi et al., 2025). Any design that softens this profile will produce a face that the target audience reads as not belonging to their community. The second establishes that traditional Emirati dress carries a semiotic function of identity demarcation in a demographically specific social context (Khalaf, 2005; Goto, 2016). The burqa, the Abuteela, and the shayla are not decorative choices. They are the sign system through which Emirati identity is encoded in the character and decoded by Emirati viewers, in Stuart Hall's (1980) terms. The third establishes that cultural authenticity and stylized visual quality are simultaneously achievable objectives, as demonstrated by the cultural validation of *Freej* with Emirati audiences over nearly two decades (Ercegovac et al., 2025; Tariq & Willard, 2022). The methodology that produced this result began with cultural research and worked outward toward stylized geometry.

The pattern of Arab misrepresentation documented in the games literature does not emerge from discriminatory intent. It emerges from the absence of methodology based on cultural evidence (Šisler, 2008; Al-Rawi, 2024). The framework proposed here provides the evidential foundation from which informed design decisions can be made. The four operational criteria derived from the converging evidence, covering nasal morphology, orbital structure, costuming, and visual system calibration, translate that foundation into production-applicable guidelines for the Endstar pipeline.

The methodology developed here, cross-domain evidential synthesis as the basis for design criteria in culturally specific character production, extends beyond the Emirati context. It offers a transferable model for any situation in which a non-dominant cultural identity is being translated into interactive media.
